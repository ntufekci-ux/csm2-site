ECA content is a structuring variable, not a procurement afterthought

Where equipment and services come from decides which agencies can support a project, and for how much.

In brief
  • Export credit support is sized on the export contract, so sourcing decisions set the size of the facility.
  • Agencies apply different content rules. The lead agency and the sourcing plan have to be chosen together.
  • Fix the content strategy before the EPC package is locked, and write it into the contracts.

An export credit agency supports exports from its own country. That simple fact has a consequence that is often noticed late: the origin of goods and services is more than a procurement matter. It determines which agencies can take part, how large the supported facility can be, and on what terms.

The arithmetic

For agencies that follow the OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits, the general rules are these.

85%Maximum share of the export contract value that can receive official support
15%Minimum down payment by the buyer
40–50%Local costs that can be supported, as a share of the export contract value: 40% for Category I countries, 50% for Category II

Everything is measured against the export contract value. A project with a large, locally sourced civil works component and a modest imported equipment package may find that the supportable amount covers far less of total cost than the sponsor assumed. The reverse also holds: placing more eligible value inside the export contract can change the financing plan materially.

Agencies differ

Content rules are set agency by agency. German export credit guarantees, for example, can include foreign content of up to 49% without special justification. UK Export Finance, in its 2019 consultation on foreign content, described a requirement of at least 20% UK content. Other agencies set their own thresholds and their own definitions of what counts.

Tenor is affected too. Since the 2023 revision of the Arrangement, the maximum repayment term is 15 years for most transactions and up to 22 years for certain climate-friendly projects. Which agency leads, and how the project is classified, shapes the repayment profile as well as the amount.

Where it goes wrong

  • Sourcing is decided on price and technical preference alone, and the eligible content turns out to be too low for the intended agency.
  • Content is spread across several countries, so that no single agency has enough at stake to lead.
  • The EPC contract does not separate eligible and ineligible items, and every later drawdown becomes a negotiation.
  • Origin is assumed, not documented, and the evidence cannot be produced when the agency asks for it.

What to do instead

Treat content as one of the variables being solved for, alongside scope, price and schedule. In practice that means mapping realistic sourcing options against the rules of the candidate agencies before tender documents are issued; deciding which agency leads and whether others need to share cover; and writing the result into the contract structure, with a price breakdown that distinguishes export content, local costs and everything else.

The test is whether the supply chain can create eligible export value without distorting the technical solution.

The order matters. The technical solution comes first, because a project re-engineered around a financing rule tends to pay for it during operation. Between technically equivalent options, though, the one that can be financed is the better one.
