When is a project EPC+F-ready? Six questions before you approach a lender

A project does not need to be perfect at screening. It needs a credible route to close the gaps.

In brief
  • Lenders and ECAs test the same six things in every project, whatever the sector.
  • At screening, gaps are normal. Gaps without an owner are the problem.
  • Approach lenders when each open item has an owner, a deliverable and a date.

Projects tend to reach lenders at the wrong moment. Some arrive too early, as a concept with a price attached. Others arrive too late, with contracts signed and procurement fixed in a form that no export credit agency can support. In both cases the first meeting is spent discovering what is missing, and a first impression in a credit committee is hard to revise.

We use six questions to decide whether a project is ready for that meeting. They are the questions a lender, an ECA and their advisers will ask anyway. Answering them first, on the sponsor’s side of the table, turns the meeting from an examination into a negotiation.

The six questions

  1. Is the technical scope defined well enough to price and to diligence?Capacity, design basis and scope of supply need to be fixed at a level the lenders’ technical adviser can test. A CAPEX figure that still moves with every design iteration undermines the model, the contract and the cover application built on it.
  2. Who borrows, and what stands behind them?Who borrows, who owns the asset, and what balance-sheet or sovereign support exists. The financing route follows the borrower: a ministry of finance, a state-owned company with a guarantee, a corporate and a project company each lead to a different structure.
  3. What repays the debt?A budget allocation, operating cash flow, a tariff, a power purchase or off-take agreement, an escrow arrangement. Lenders finance a repayment source. The asset is how that source is created.
  4. Can the supply chain create eligible export content?ECA support follows exports. The question is whether sourcing can generate enough eligible value for the intended agency without distorting the technical solution. We cover this in a separate note.
  5. Are environmental, social, land and permitting risks on a credible path?ECAs and development finance institutions apply environmental and social standards of their own. A missing impact assessment or an unresolved land question can stop a transaction that is sound in every other respect.
  6. Can the contracts allocate risk on bankable terms?Completion, performance, interfaces between packages and payment milestones each have to sit with a party able to carry them. A delivery model that leaves interface risk with the borrower is hard to finance as drafted.

Reading the answers

No project scores full marks at screening, and it does not need to. What separates a financeable project from a stalled one is whether each gap has a route to closure: a study commissioned, a guarantee under discussion, a sourcing plan being tested against agency rules.

We therefore look at each question in three states: where the project stands at screening, what the route to close looks like, and what must be true at financial close. The profile above is illustrative. The point it makes is general: the six areas do not mature at the same speed, and the slowest one sets the date of financial close.

At screening, gaps are normal. Gaps without an owner are the problem.

When to make the approach

The right moment is when every open item has an owner, a deliverable and a date, and when the answers are consistent with one another. A repayment source that assumes a sovereign guarantee, next to a borrower structure that assumes none, tells a lender more about the project than any presentation.

If that consistency is not there yet, the budget is better spent on closing gaps than on a roadshow.

This note sets out CSM2 Finance’s general approach. It is not legal or financial advice, and each transaction turns on its own facts.
