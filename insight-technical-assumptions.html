A financial model is only as strong as the technical assumptions behind it

Lenders test the engineering before they trust the numbers. Sponsors should test it first.

In brief
  • Capex, schedule, output and operating costs in the model all come from technical work.
  • Lenders appoint an independent engineer to check those inputs. Weak assumptions surface there, late and expensively.
  • Review the technical basis before the model goes to lenders, not after.

A financial model turns assumptions into cash flows. The spreadsheet can be flawless and the project still unfinanceable, because the inputs that matter most, such as construction cost, schedule, output, availability and operating cost, come from engineering, not from finance.

What the independent engineer checks

In project and structured financings, lenders typically appoint an independent engineer, also called the lenders’ technical advisor, to review the technical basis of the project. A World Bank sample terms of reference for a wind farm shows the usual scope:

  • The design, the suitability of the main equipment for the site, the origin of components and the manufacturer’s track record.
  • Energy yield, with loss factors and uncertainty levels at P50, P90 and other probability levels.
  • The construction schedule and milestones, the reasonableness of the cost estimate, and cost overrun scenarios.
  • The EPC, supply, operation and maintenance, and offtake contracts, for technical adequacy and consistency with each other.
  • The technical inputs to the financial model, with sensitivity scenarios.
  • Permits, grid connection, commissioning and performance testing.

The same sample allows nine weeks for the first phase of this review. Findings that change capex, schedule or output at that stage change the debt size, the reserve accounts and sometimes the structure itself.

Where assumptions usually break

  • Capex built on early estimates, without a defined scope or a contingency for ground and site conditions.
  • Schedules that ignore permitting, procurement lead times or seasonal constraints.
  • Output or availability figures taken from equipment brochures instead of a site-specific assessment.
  • Operating costs that leave out major maintenance and spare parts.
  • Contracts whose guarantees and liquidated damages do not match what the model assumes.

Test first, then model

The practical answer is to put the technical basis through a lender-style review before the model is shared: check the maturity of the scope, challenge the cost estimate, align the schedule with procurement and permits, and make sure the contracts back up the assumptions. Many advisors can build a financial model. Fewer can challenge the engineering behind it.

Lenders test the engineering before they trust the numbers.
Sources

Scope of lenders’ technical due diligence: World Bank PPP Legal Resource Center, Terms of Reference: Technical Due Diligence, Wind Power Project (sample), accessed November 2026.
