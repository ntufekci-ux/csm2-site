Choosing the financing route: start from the transaction, not the lender

Sovereign, corporate, project or ECA-supported: the borrower, the repayment source and the supply chain decide which route can close.

In brief
  • There are four basic routes: sovereign or public, corporate balance sheet, project or limited recourse, and ECA-supported or blended.
  • The route follows from who borrows and what repays the debt. Approaching lenders before those are clear costs time.
  • ECA, IFI and DFI support is an overlay on the borrower and repayment structure, not a separate world.

A common first question is “which bank will finance this?” A better first question is “who borrows, and what repays the debt?” The answers usually narrow the field to one or two financing routes, and they decide which lenders and agencies are worth approaching.

Four routes

  • Sovereign or public. A ministry, public agency or state-owned company borrows, usually with a sovereign guarantee or a budget-backed repayment source. Appetite depends on the country’s risk and debt capacity.
  • Corporate or balance sheet. A private borrower supported by its own credit, a parent guarantee, assets and covenants. Simpler to document, but limited by the size of the balance sheet.
  • Project or limited recourse. Debt raised by a special purpose vehicle and repaid mainly from project cash flows, supported by offtake, concession rights and project-level security. Possible only when revenues are contracted and risks are clearly allocated.
  • ECA-supported or blended. Buyer credit, export credit cover, IFI or DFI participation, political risk insurance, or a mix of these, layered onto one of the three routes above.

The questions that decide it

  • Who borrows, and who owns the asset?
  • What ultimately repays the debt: a public budget, corporate cash flow, a tariff, a power purchase agreement, an offtake contract or an escrow account?
  • What does the country’s risk profile allow, for commercial lenders and for agencies?
  • Where will equipment and services come from, and which agencies does that bring into play?
  • What security can be offered: guarantees, assets, assigned revenues, reserve accounts, insurance?

Agencies are an overlay

Export credit agencies, IFIs and DFIs do not replace the borrower or the repayment source; they sit on top of them. UK Export Finance, for example, says its buyer credit facility can support corporate, sovereign and public buyers, and lists limited recourse project finance and public-private partnerships among the structures it can back. An agency changes the risk profile of a loan. It does not create a borrower or a revenue stream where none exists.

The financing route is selected around the transaction, not the other way around.

Common mistakes

  • Starting from a lender’s term sheet and reshaping the project to fit it.
  • Assuming project finance is available for a project without contracted revenues.
  • Treating a sovereign guarantee as a formality, when it needs formal approval from the ministry of finance and takes time to obtain.
  • Fixing the supply chain before checking which agencies it brings into play.

Once the route is clear, the rest of the work becomes specific: which documents lenders will need, which agency leads, how the security package is built and how long it will take to close.
